# CRO Rules: Trades - Roofing, Plumbing, Electrical

Derived from `04-cro-patterns.md` and the niche's top-of-pool reference analysis. Stage 10.4b reads these as QA gates on top of the universal CRO rules.

---

## 1. Above-the-Fold Rules

```
Rule: Phone number visible in the nav bar on every page.
Evidence: 100% of top-performing roofing sites (all 8 captured) show click-to-call in the header. 
The phone call is the primary conversion in this niche. Homeowners call first; they fill forms second.
Failure mode: If the phone number is absent from the nav, the homeowner who wants to call (majority) 
has no path. Bounce rate spikes for the call-intent segment.

Rule: Primary CTA button above the fold. Accent background, ink text, Montserrat 700.
Evidence: Every top-performer places the CTA button in the hero, not just in the hero image area.
Failure mode: If the CTA is below the fold or only in the nav, the homeowner who is ready 
to act has to scroll before they can act. Conversion rate drops.

Rule: Review count visible above or inside the hero.
Evidence: 7 of 8 top-performing sites show a star + review count in the hero or immediately 
below the headline. The homeowner's first filter is "how many reviews?"
Failure mode: If social proof is deferred to a lower section, the homeowner completes their 
trust scan elsewhere (Google, BBB) before the page has established credibility. 
Brand impression is set before the site can set it.

Rule: Certification signal (GAF / BBB) visible above the fold.
Evidence: 6 of 8 top-performers show a certification badge in the hero or trust bar, 
visible without scrolling.
Failure mode: Deferred certification signals mean the homeowner has already formed a trust 
judgment before they reach them. First impressions dominate.
```

## 2. Trust Signal Density

```
Rule: Trust bar immediately below the hero. 5 items. No scroll required.
Evidence: The trust bar is a universal pattern across top-performing roofing sites. 
All 8 captured sites place it directly below the hero, before the services section.
Failure mode: If the trust bar is placed mid-page or in the footer only, the homeowner 
who arrived via Google Maps is already forming a judgment based on the hero alone. 
The trust bar is the first section that survives a 3-second scan.

Rule: Each badge in the trust bar is labelled with a plain-English descriptor.
Evidence: Homeowners do not know what GAF, BBB, or NRCA mean. The badge alone is not enough.
Failure mode: An unlabelled badge is visual noise. It does not communicate trust; 
it just takes up space.

Rule: BBB badge links to the actual BBB profile (external link, opens in new tab).
Evidence: Homeowners who check BBB expect to verify the rating. A dead or absent link 
breaks the verification path.
Failure mode: If the link is missing, the homeowner who wants to verify goes to BBB.org 
directly. If they cannot find the profile, they move to the next contractor.

Rule: License number displayed as text, not just the word "licensed."
Evidence: Homeowners are told to ask for the license number. Displaying it removes that 
friction point and signals nothing to hide.
Failure mode: "Licensed and Insured" without the actual number is unverifiable. 
Some homeowners treat this as a red flag.
```

## 3. Form Friction

```
Rule: Lead form placed in the hero, not on a separate contact page.
Evidence: 6 of 8 top-performers embed the quote form directly in the hero, 
above or adjacent to the headline. The form-in-hero pattern outperforms the contact-page 
redirect because the homeowner does not have to navigate away.
Failure mode: Routing to a contact page creates one additional click and one additional 
page load. Every extra step loses a portion of the audience.

Rule: Required form fields: Full Name, Phone Number, Service Needed (dropdown), Email.
Maximum 4-5 fields.
Evidence: The 4-field pattern is the standard across top-performing roofing sites. 
The service dropdown qualifies the lead before the contractor calls back.
Failure mode: More than 5 fields increases abandonment significantly. 
Fewer than 4 fields under-qualifies leads and wastes the contractor's time.

Rule: Phone is a required field.
Evidence: Roofing leads convert via phone call. An email-only lead is low-intent.
Failure mode: If phone is optional, the contractor receives unworkable leads.

Rule: Address collection happens post-submit (in the callback), not in the form.
Evidence: Asking for an address in the form signals a high-commitment request. 
Homeowners who are still deciding are more likely to abandon.
Failure mode: Adding address to the form reduces completions without adding qualifying value.

Rule: Submit button text is "Get My Free Quote" not "Submit."
Evidence: First-person copy ("my") consistently outperforms generic submit text. 
The button should reinforce what the homeowner is getting, not describe the action they are taking.
Failure mode: Generic submit labels ("Submit", "Send") reduce perceived value of the click.
```

## 4. Mobile-Specific Rules

```
Rule: Sticky bottom CTA bar present on mobile. 56px tall. Accent background.
Evidence: 7 of 8 top-performing sites use a fixed bottom bar on mobile. 
Roofing homeowners on mobile call first. The bar must be accessible at all scroll positions.
Failure mode: Without the bottom bar, the call CTA is only visible when the homeowner 
is at the top of the page or actively scrolling back up.

Rule: Click-to-call is the primary CTA on mobile. Form is secondary.
Evidence: Mobile conversion in roofing is overwhelmingly phone calls. 
The form is present but secondary.
Failure mode: If the form is equally prominent as click-to-call on mobile, the experience 
is optimised for the minority use case.

Rule: Form fields are full-width on mobile (100% width, min 44px tap target).
Evidence: Narrow form fields on mobile cause input errors and frustration.
Failure mode: Sub-44px tap targets fail WCAG 2.1 AA and hurt conversions.

Rule: Trust bar wraps to 2-column grid on screens below 480px (rows of 2 + 1 centered).
Evidence: A 5-item trust bar does not compress into a readable single row below 480px 
without items becoming too small to read or tap.
Failure mode: Squashed trust bar items are unreadable. The trust signal is destroyed.
```

## 5. Pricing Transparency

```
Rule: No price listed on the homepage or above the fold. Quote-only.
Evidence: Roofing prices vary enormously by house size, material, and damage extent. 
A stated price that is lower than the final quote destroys trust. A stated price that 
is higher loses homeowners before they contact.
Failure mode: Premature pricing anchors the conversation incorrectly and generates 
low-quality leads who argue about the estimate before the inspection.

Rule: Pricing guidance on service pages only. Format: "Costs vary by roof size, material, 
and existing damage. Get a written quote after your free inspection."
Evidence: Service pages attract homeowners in research mode who want some information. 
Refusing to discuss cost at all reads as evasive.
Failure mode: No pricing language at all frustrates research-mode homeowners. 
They will find a competitor who at least gives them a framework.

Rule: Financing mentioned on service pages and contact page if the contractor offers it.
Evidence: High-ticket services ($5,000-$20,000+) convert better when financing is available 
and clearly communicated.
Failure mode: Not mentioning financing loses homeowners whose budget concern is about 
cash flow, not total cost.
```

## 6. Review Presentation

```
Rule: Review source is Google (primary), with BBB as secondary.
Evidence: Homeowners check Google reviews as their first filter. 
BBB accreditation is the second filter. Facebook reviews are supplementary.
Failure mode: Leading with Facebook or Houzz reviews when Google reviews exist 
mismatches the homeowner's verification pathway.

Rule: Review excerpt length: 180 characters max on the card. Expand link for full review.
Evidence: Cards with long reviews cause visual imbalance in the grid. 
Homeowners scan reviews; they do not read full blocks.
Failure mode: Full-length review cards create an uneven grid and slow the scan. 
Homeowners absorb less when each item takes longer to parse.

Rule: Reviewer photo required on homepage testimonials.
Evidence: Named testimonials with a photo convert significantly better than name-only cards. 
The photo signals a real person, not an invented review.
Failure mode: Name-only testimonials are distrusted in this niche because fabricated 
reviews are a known problem. A photo adds the accountability signal.

Rule: Review velocity proof: show total count in hero bar and again as the section 
heading subtext.
Evidence: The count (200+, 500+) is the primary filter homeowners use before visiting 
the site. Showing it in the hero bar and again in the reviews section reinforces 
the signal throughout the page.
Failure mode: Showing the count only once reduces the number of times the homeowner 
processes the social proof signal.
```

## 7. Process and Service Explanation

```
Rule: Process section uses exactly 3 steps: Free Inspection / Custom Quote / Expert Installation.
Evidence: 3-step process is universal across the top-performing roofing sites in the pool. 
Research confirms this is the niche standard. 4 or 5 steps adds cognitive load 
without adding trust.
Failure mode: 4+ steps makes the process seem more complicated than it is. 
The homeowner's fear is commitment; a longer process amplifies that fear.

Rule: Process section includes a "No Obligation" badge or subtext below the steps.
Evidence: The homeowner's top fear is commitment. "No obligation" directly addresses it.
Failure mode: Omitting the no-obligation signal leaves the process section as a sales pitch 
rather than a trust-builder.

Rule: Service page minimum word count: 600 words.
Evidence: Google's local service pages with 600+ words of unique content outrank 
thin pages for city + service keyword combinations.
Failure mode: Sub-300 word service pages do not rank for competitive terms and 
do not give the homeowner enough information to make a decision.

Rule: Service page includes FAQ section with FAQPage schema.
Evidence: FAQ schema generates rich results in Google SERPs. Roofing FAQ questions 
have high search volume and low competition at the question level.
Failure mode: No FAQ section misses the long-tail informational traffic 
and the rich-result placement.
```

## 8. Quantified-Trust Line

```
Pattern 1 (10+ years, 100+ reviews):
Conditions: years_in_business >= 10 AND total_review_count >= 100
Output template: "{years} years protecting homeowners in {primaryCity}"
Example: "14 years protecting homeowners in Nashville"

Pattern 2 (100+ jobs, 3+ years):
Conditions: roofs_installed >= 100 AND years_in_business >= 3
Output template: "{jobCount}+ roofs installed across {region} since {foundedYear}"
Example: "400+ roofs installed across the Atlanta metro since 2012"

Pattern 3 (certified, review count):
Conditions: gaf_master_elite == true AND total_review_count >= 50
Output template: "GAF Master Elite certified. {reviewCount} five-star reviews in {primaryCity}."
Example: "GAF Master Elite certified. 183 five-star reviews in Phoenix."

Pattern 4 (multi-area, review count):
Conditions: service_areas.length >= 5 AND total_review_count >= 20
Output template: "{reviewCount} verified reviews. Serving {city} and {N} surrounding cities."
Example: "96 verified reviews. Serving Denver and 8 surrounding cities."

Pattern 5 (fallback):
Conditions: (always)
Output template: "Serving {primaryCity} homeowners since {foundedYear}"
Example: "Serving Dallas homeowners since 2009"
```

See `quantified-trust-templates.md` for the full pattern list with placeholders.

## 9. Conversion-Killer Anti-Patterns

```
Anti-pattern: Form on a separate contact page, not in the hero.
Why it fails: Creates a navigation step between the homeowner and their intent. 
Every extra click loses conversions. The highest-performing roofing sites embed 
the form directly in the hero.

Anti-pattern: Phone number only in the footer, not in the nav bar.
Why it fails: The homeowner's instinct in this niche is to call. If the number 
is not immediately visible, the homeowner who wants to call has to hunt for it. 
Some will give up and try the next result.

Anti-pattern: Trust bar placed mid-page after the services section.
Why it fails: Trust must be established before the homeowner reads the pitch. 
Placing the trust bar after the services section reverses the decision flow. 
The homeowner is being asked to evaluate services before they know if the contractor is legit.

Anti-pattern: Hero headline that is a brand slogan, not a proof statement.
Why it fails: "Your Roof, Our Priority" tells the homeowner nothing. 
"247 Five-Star Reviews. Nashville's Most Trusted Roofing Contractor." tells them everything. 
Slogans replace proof. Proof converts.

Anti-pattern: No license number visible anywhere on the site.
Why it fails: Homeowners are told to ask for the license number. 
A site without one visible raises the question: why are they hiding it? 
Top-performing sites display the number in the trust bar and the footer.

Anti-pattern: Generic stock photos of houses (no crew, no real job site).
Why it fails: "I wanted to see photos of actual roofs they'd done, not stock images." 
(Customer verbatim, sub-task 2). Stock photos signal low investment and raise 
authenticity doubts. Real crew photos are a direct CRO driver in this niche.

Anti-pattern: Reviews section with generic star ratings but no named testimonials.
Why it fails: Homeowners distrust anonymous reviews after high-profile fake review scandals. 
Named testimonials with photos + outcomes are the conversion mechanism, 
not aggregate star counts.
```
